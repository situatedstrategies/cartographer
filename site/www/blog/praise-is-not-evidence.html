Praise is not evidence: appraising an agent's work honestly

A confident summary feels like a finished job. It's a claim, though, and a claim still needs checking.

By Team Cartographer · October 5th 2026 · 4 minute read

We know the feeling. The terminal goes quiet, a tidy summary scrolls past, and it ends with "all done." Something in us relaxes. We nod, maybe say thanks, and move on.

That relaxed feeling is worth a second look. It's a reaction to tone, and tone isn't evidence of anything. Here's what some research says about why we're so ready to accept automated output, and what we can do about it.

Leaning on the machine is an old habit

Researchers have a name for it: automation bias, the tendency to over-rely on automation.1 A 2012 systematic review by Kate Goddard, Abdul Roudsari and Jeremy Wyatt looked at it across several research fields, with a focus on clinical decision support in healthcare. Of 13,821 papers retrieved, 74 met their inclusion criteria.1

One observation from that review stays with us. Although most research shows overall improved performance with these tools, there is often a failure to recognize the new errors the tools can introduce.1 The help is real, and so is the blind spot it creates. Both are true at once.

The review also found that how much people lean on automation isn't fixed. Factors such as cognitive style, task specific experience, trust and confidence all played a part, and so did workload, task complexity and time constraints.1 Notice that last group. Busy, hurried people lean harder. That describes most of us at the end of a long session.

Feeling sure and being right are different things

A second study is closer to our own world. Neil Perry, Megha Srivastava, Deepak Kumar and Dan Boneh ran a user study of people writing code with and without an AI assistant. They found that participants with access to an AI assistant wrote significantly less secure code than those without.2 They also found that the assisted participants were more likely to believe they had written secure code.2

The authors suggest this means such tools may lead users to be overconfident about security flaws in their code.2 That's their reading, and it's worth keeping it that modest. The study was about security tasks and one particular assistant, not about every kind of work. It also noted something encouraging: participants who trusted the AI less and engaged more with their prompts wrote code with fewer vulnerabilities.2

Neither study tells us how any given coding agent behaves, and we won't claim they do. What they show is on the human side of the screen. We can feel secure about work that isn't. And when something automated is helping, we're prone to miss the errors it adds.

Praise is a mood, not a measurement

So here's our own view, offered as a view and not as a finding. A summary that says "all done" is a statement about the work, written by the one who did it. Sometimes it's right. But it's still only a statement. It can't tell you whether the thing you meant is the thing you got.

When a person tells us "it's finished," we usually ask to see it. We should extend the same courtesy to a tool, and to ourselves when we're feeling pleased. A few habits help:

  • Run it. Open the page, call the function, feed it the awkward input. Watching it work beats reading that it works.
  • Compare it to what you asked for. Reread your original request, not the summary of it.
  • Ask what would prove it wrong. If nothing could, you haven't tested anything yet.
  • Look for what the summary skipped. The omissions are often where the trouble sits.
  • Slow down when you're rushed. That's when leaning is strongest.

None of this is distrust. It's the ordinary habit of checking work before we sign for it, and it's kinder to our future selves than finding out in production.

Where a record helps

There's one more piece. To compare the result with what you meant, you need to remember what you meant, and what happened along the way. Cartographer reads the session history your coding agent already keeps and tells each session back to you as a story: what you did, what happened, and how you responded. It reads what's already on your machine, with no cloud. It's a way to look at the build instead of only at its closing summary.

A smaller kind of honesty

We think appraising work honestly is mostly a matter of keeping two questions apart. "Does this sound finished?" and "Is this what we meant?" feel alike in the moment. Only the second one is evidence. It takes a minute to ask, and it's usually the cheapest minute of the whole project.
