Feeling fast versus being fast: timing your agent-assisted work

We're all poor judges of our own pace. Here's what a controlled trial and a classic study say, and a small habit for checking.

By Team Cartographer · October 25th 2026 · 4 minute read

Ask someone at the end of a long agent session whether it saved them time and they'll almost always say yes. It feels true. Things appeared on screen quickly, and we weren't staring at a blank file.

This is a bit of a musing about that feeling. We're not here to say agents are slow. We're curious how well any of us can tell.

One trial that split the feeling from the clock

In 2025, the research group METR ran a randomized controlled trial with experienced open-source developers. According to the paper, 16 developers completed 246 tasks in mature projects they already worked on, and each task was randomly assigned to allow AI tools or not.1

Before starting, the developers forecast that allowing AI would reduce completion time by 24%. Afterwards they estimated it had reduced time by 20%. The measured result went the other way: allowing AI increased completion time by 19%.1

That's a striking gap between how the work felt and how long it took. It's worth holding it loosely, though. METR describes the result as a snapshot of early-2025 AI capabilities in one setting, and the study covers a small group working in codebases they knew well.2 It doesn't tell us how your project goes. What it does tell us is that the feeling of speed and the measurement of speed can come apart, even for experienced people.

This is an old habit of mind

Psychologists noticed something similar long before coding agents. In a set of studies of what's called the planning fallacy, Buehler, Griffin and Ross reported that people underestimate their own task completion times but not other people's, and that they lean on plan-based scenarios more than on their past experience.3

We find that oddly comforting. Misjudging our own pace isn't a failing of the tools or of us in particular. It's how estimating works when we're the ones inside the story.

Why a session feels quicker than it was

We can only offer a guess here, and it's ours, not a finding. Agent work gives you lots of small, visible progress: a file appears, a test runs, a summary lands. The waiting, the re-prompting, the reading of output and the repair of a wrong turn are quieter. They're easy to forget by evening.

If that's roughly right, the fix isn't to feel worse about the work. It's to stop relying on the feeling alone.

Fast at what, exactly?

Part of the trouble is that "faster" hides several questions. Did the first draft arrive sooner? Did the working version? Did the version you'd happily hand to someone else? Those can have very different answers, and a clock that stops at the first one flatters the whole session.

The trial above measured the time to finish each task. It doesn't say anything about quality, learning, or how pleasant the work was, and we wouldn't want to claim it does. Some of those may matter more to you than minutes. The point is only that you can't weigh them if you've never looked at the minutes at all.

So we try to keep the question narrow. Not "is this better?" but "what did this task cost me, start to finished, and was that what I expected?" Narrow questions are easier to answer honestly.

Where Cartographer fits

Cartographer reads the session history your coding agent already keeps and turns it into a map of how a project was built. It runs on your machine, with no cloud. We mention it only because a record you didn't write from memory is a kinder witness than recollection.

A small habit

Before a task, write down a guess in minutes. After it, write down when you actually started and finished. Do it for a handful of tasks, with the agent and without. You're not building a study. You're just giving your future self something better than a feeling to look at, and the gap between the two numbers is usually the most interesting part.
