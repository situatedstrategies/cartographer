Why a correction is the most informative prompt in a session

Most prompts say what you want. A correction shows where what you wanted and what you got came apart. That makes it worth a second look.

By Team Cartographer · October 11th 2026 · 4 minute read

Scroll back through a long session and most of it blurs. Requests, replies, more requests. Then you hit one of your own messages that begins "No, not that" or "I meant the other file", and it stops you. Something happened there. We'd argue it's the most informative line in the whole transcript.

What a correction carries

An ordinary prompt is a hope. It describes an outcome you haven't seen yet. A correction is different, because it's a judgment made against something real. You looked at what came back, compared it with what you meant, and found a gap. Then you wrote the gap down.

So a single correction holds three things at once. It holds what you meant, which was often fuzzier in your head than in your first prompt. It holds what the agent took you to mean. And it holds the exact place where the two split. Few other lines in a session do that much work in so few words.

To be clear, that's our reading, not a finding. No study we know of measures "information in a correction." What the research does show is narrower, and it points the same way.

What the research says about fixing errors

Suzhen Zhong, Ying Zou and Bram Adams studied real conversations between developers and a large language model, building a dataset called CodeChat from them. Among their findings: prompts that point out mistakes in code generated in prior turns and explicitly request a fix are most effective for resolving errors.1

Read that closely. The effective move isn't a louder "try again." It names the mistake and asks for the fix. That's a correction doing its job: it carries the gap along with it, so the next attempt has something to aim at.

The same paper reports that syntax and import errors persist across turns of a conversation.1 Problems don't always melt away on their own. Somebody has to notice them and say so, and that somebody is usually you.

What the vendor docs say about timing

Anthropic's best practices guide for Claude Code is a vendor document, so it speaks only for that tool. We cite it for what it says, not for anything about how any model works inside. The guide advises that you correct Claude as soon as you notice it going off track, and says the best results come from tight feedback loops.2

It also names a failure pattern worth knowing. The guide says that if you've corrected Claude more than twice on the same issue in one session, the context is cluttered with failed approaches, and it suggests clearing and starting again with a more specific prompt that uses what you learned.2

Put the two together and a correction has a second job. Besides steering the next reply, it tells you something about your own prompt. One correction is normal. Three on the same point is a sign the original ask was missing something, and the pile of corrections is the evidence of what.

Reading your corrections afterwards

Here's a small exercise. After a session, skip everything else and read only your corrections, in order. A few patterns tend to show up.

  • The same correction twice. Something you assumed was obvious never made it into a prompt or a project note.
  • A correction that changes direction. Your goal moved, and the first prompt no longer describes it.
  • A correction that narrows scope. You were clear about what to do, and less clear about what to leave alone.
  • A correction you almost didn't make. Those are the ones to be honest about, because they mark where you nearly accepted something you didn't understand.

None of these labels come from a study. They're what we notice when we reread our own sessions. Your list may look different, and that's fine. The point is that corrections are already sorted for you: they're the moments you disagreed.

A habit worth keeping

Next time you type "no, I meant", pause for a second and finish the thought. What did you mean? What did the agent assume instead? If you can say both in a sentence, put that sentence in the correction. The research suggests naming the mistake helps, and it'll help you later when you read the record back.

A correction can feel like friction, a small tax on getting things done. We'd rather think of it as the session telling you the truth about itself.
