Declaring done before you start: goals as a yardstick

Most of us start with the task and discover the finish line later. Research on goals, and a habit from agile teams, both suggest doing it the other way round.

By Team Cartographer · October 7th 2026 · 4 minute read

You ask for a feature. The agent works, the tests go green, the summary says it's finished. Is it? That depends on a question you may never have answered: finished compared to what?

If the answer only forms after the work is on your screen, you end up judging the result by how it feels. A tidy diff feels done. A confident summary feels done. Neither is a yardstick. This post is about setting the yardstick first.

What goal research found

Edwin Locke and Gary Latham spent decades studying how goals shape work. In a 2002 review of 35 years of their research, they report that specific, difficult goals consistently led to higher performance than urging people to do their best.1 "Do your best" sounds encouraging. It's also a goal nobody can fail, because it never says what success is.

The same review is careful about conditions. The advantage holds when people get feedback about their progress, are committed to the goal, and have the ability and knowledge to do the task.1 A goal does two jobs here. It directs effort, and it gives you something to measure against as you go.

That research is about people doing work, mostly people doing their own. It isn't about coding agents, and we won't claim it is. What follows is our reading of it, not a finding.

What agile teams call it

Software teams reached for the same idea under a different name. In Scrum, the Definition of Done is a written statement of what it takes for work to count as complete. Scrum.org's write-up says that work that fails the Definition of Done isn't released or shown at the Sprint Review and goes back to the Product Backlog.2

Notice what it does for a team. Nobody has to argue, afterwards, about whether something is finished. The argument happened earlier, when it was cheap.

Why it matters more with an agent

When you write the code yourself, you carry the goal in your head and adjust it as you go. When something else writes it, your head isn't in the loop in the same way. You see a result and have to decide what to make of it.

Without a stated finish line, three things tend to happen:

  • The goal drifts. Each new prompt nudges it, and nobody notices the sum.
  • "Done" becomes whatever the agent reports. Its summary becomes the yardstick, which makes it both the author and the judge.
  • Extras slip in. Without edges, anything that works looks like progress.

None of that is anyone's fault. It's what happens when there's nothing to compare against.

What a good yardstick looks like

It doesn't need to be long. A few lines before you start will do:

  • The outcome, in terms someone could observe. "Users can reset their password by email," not "improve login."
  • What would show it works. A test, a command you can run, a page you can open.
  • What's out of scope. Naming the edges stops the slow creep.
  • What you'd accept as good enough. Specific doesn't have to mean perfect.

Then comes the part people skip: compare the result against what you wrote, not against what you now remember wanting. Memory is generous to whatever got built.

You're also allowed to revise the yardstick. Plans change, and sometimes the build teaches you what you actually wanted. Just change it on purpose, in writing, rather than letting it slide.

A small habit to try

Before your next prompt, write one sentence that starts with "This is done when." If you can't finish it, that's useful news, and it arrived before any code did. If you can, you've given yourself something the summary can't talk you out of.
